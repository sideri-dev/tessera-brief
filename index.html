Japan's interior/architecture fee standard update just got sharper — four named mechanism changes and the technical advisory number behind them.

The fee standard that replaced Japan's old design-fee benchmark was already confirmed here earlier this month. A 186-page government guideline now names the four things that actually changed: the simplified-fee table revised to cover detached houses, the difficulty-correction method revised, the multi-use-building workload calculation revised, and full mandatory compliance with energy-efficiency standards built in — implemented via technical advisory notice 307, dated January 9, 2026.

If a proposal call or competition secretariat is still citing the old standard or the old simplified-fee table, this is the correction to hand them — the energy-code and detached-house changes are specific enough to move an actual fee calculation, not just a citation.

Every one of five named contract-furniture houses takes an outside designer strictly by invitation — no open call, no supplier-application page, no published terms on any of them. Not one publishes a fee, royalty rate, minimum volume, tooling cost or development timeline; the only published design-specific royalty figure in Japan is a professional-body model-contract worked example (7%/5%/3% stepped on ex-factory price), which sits on a different denominator from the only government royalty dataset and isn't market data.

Since January 2026, a newly enforced subcontracting-fairness law's employee-count threshold catches every house in this set and now requires IP scope to be priced separately in the order, or it counts as an unfair low-balling practice. Real drawing-to-catalogue timelines run 2–3 years — the short lead times this sector is known for measure catalogue customisation and parts orders, not development.

This week

The fire-code wall around interior materials has a door, and it's a room-geometry decision made before any material is chosen. Compartmenting hotel guestrooms to 100㎡ or less inside a fire-resistant structure drops them out of interior-finish restrictions entirely — corridors and stairs get no equivalent relief — and museums get a parallel exemption for compartments 100㎡ or less with a 3m+ ceiling. The certification alternative runs roughly ¥540,000–¥930,000 and six-plus months; the geometry move is free if it's decided at the basic-planning stage, ruinous if discovered later.

Named craft regions are being specified into hospitality without a maker, a lead time, or a price attached, while supply behind each name is measurably shrinking. One recently reopened hotel's guestroom art has exactly one named technique on record (a decorator painting salvaged brick) and no maker named at all; meanwhile gold-leaf raw material rose 17% in a month, one major lacquerware region sits at 60–70% of pre-earthquake capacity two and a half years on, and structural glue-laminated timber imports keep falling. The one clean counter-example on file is a hotel where the papermaker is named alongside the certification that made the paper specifiable — that's the difference between a mood-board reference and a procurement route.

Two live Japanese public commissions this week opened for exactly the scope the national fee standard doesn't cover, and neither published a price. Exhibition design, art selection and furniture/fixture selection appear zero times in the fee standard's own explanatory material — there's no base rate to work from, so whatever comparator a designer brings to the room is the number.
