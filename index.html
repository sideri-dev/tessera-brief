Fukuoka's Asian Art Museum has put a disclosed, government-primary fee ceiling on the basic design of its new underground wing — ¥191,980,000, all-in.

Fukuoka City's own procurement notice for the museum's underground expansion (a converted former Kego Park car park in Tenjin) opens a two-stage open-call proposal process with a checkable eligibility bar — a licensed architecture office with a completed 1,000㎡+ new-build, extension or renovation design job in the past ten years. The city's own field label reads "estimate ceiling price," not "planned price" — a single tax-inclusive ceiling rather than a scored price band, corrected here to the all-in tax-inclusive budget-ceiling type.

A separate ¥14bn construction-cost figure and a "joint-venture up to five firms" detail could not be confirmed on the fetched pages this cycle and are dropped rather than published unverified. This is the strongest disclosed public comparator for scaling a basic-design-only fee on a museum-scale cultural commission currently in the corpus.

Kamakura's Mikawaya Honten renovation just cleared Japan's first-ever exemption letting a wooden building keep its wooden structure inside a fire-prevention zone.

The developer's own release confirms the milestone verbatim as a first-ever case nationally, under Article 3, Paragraph 1, Item 3 of the Building Standards Act — the culmination of an eight-year, multi-institution process running since 2018 with the city, the fire department, the University of Tokyo (structural), Yokohama National University (historical) and the Japan Association of Structural Engineers (third-party seismic evaluation) all named participants.

The building is 605㎡ of site, 460㎡ of floor area, construction started November 2024, architect Araki+Sasaki Architects. What this hands a practitioner is a named, defensible legal route — not a workaround, a cleared precedent — for taking a wooden heritage building inside a fire-prevention zone to commercial hospitality use without stripping its wooden fabric.

A live certification-number conflict on a rammed-earth-look plaster is resolved — and the resolution finds a company's own blog was quoting a gypsum-board certification by mistake.

Harada Sakan's live catalogue page states its rammed-earth-look sprayed finish carries certification number NM-8571 (noncombustible), grouped with NM-9811 (quasi-noncombustible) and NM-9366 (flame-resistant). A company blog post instead cited NM-8619 — and an independent certification-database check confirms NM-8619 actually belongs to the Gypsum Board Industry Association's gypsum board, an unrelated product entirely. A third-party paint manufacturer's own current fire-material list independently corroborates the NM-8571/9811/9366 set exactly as Harada's catalogue has it.

The standard price on the page runs from ¥45,000/㎡ at 10mm coat thickness (tax basis unstated, so not quoted as ex-tax). The upshot: a factory-certified, fire-rated substitute for a rammed-earth aesthetic, now with a settled and independently checkable certification number rather than two contradictory numbers in circulation.

A 130-year-old tea warehouse in Fujieda became a recording studio via a full building lift-and-reseat, and it's hitting Dr65–75 sound isolation.

The project team's own construction log describes lifting the 130-year earthen storehouse by crane, jacking it slowly, and pouring a new reinforced foundation beneath it — carried out by Shizuoka's own temple carpenters — with base-isolation rubber under active consideration to further damp the structure's own vibration. An independent site-visit report confirms the building is hitting a Dr65–75 acoustic target, with a specific fix for flutter echo: the rear wall's lower section is deliberately angled to break up reflection between the entrance door and the back wall.

One claim in the original write-up — describing the result as an independent box-in-box structure — couldn't be confirmed on any fetched source and is dropped; what survives is a verified, quantified method for turning a wooden storehouse into a sound-critical space without losing the original fabric.

Frieze Seoul and Kiaf just closed with primary-market prices for contemporary work clearing from about $1,000 up to a $1.8m top sale.

At the top: Yoo Youngkuk's 1971 painting sold at Kukje Gallery for KRW 2.2–2.5bn (around $1.6m–1.8m). Mid-tier galleries posted dealer-ask ranges rather than confirmed sales — Kim Tschang-yeul priced $180,000–$1.1m, Seung-taek Lee $170,000–$700,000, both at Gallery Hyundai; Thaddaeus Ropac sold an Adrian Ghenie painting for €1.1m ($1.28m) and an Antony Gormley sculpture for £750,000 ($1m).

At the accessible end, Kiaf booth prices ran roughly $1,000–$40,000 — a figure corrected here after an initial write-up conflated it with a narrower $8,700–$35,000 preview-sale figure for three works by one Japanese artist at one gallery. That accessible band is the number worth keeping on file for emerging-to-established contemporary art procurement on a hospitality project.

Last dossier

The 民藝 (mingei, folk-craft) market has no measurable price series since 2020 because it has no category to measure: Japan's highest-volume auction house returns zero hits for the term on its own genre page, and every institutional attendance or acquisition figure is unpublished. The one real number in the whole run is a matched pair from a furniture maker's own price list six years apart — every matched product rose, none held flat or fell, median roughly +19–20% ex-tax depending on parse.

Demand is real and measured indirectly: two major museum shows both beat their attendance plans, and at a craft retailer foreign visitors are 38% of footfall but 49% of purchases at over double the per-head spend. But supply, not demand, is the actual constraint — the flagship mingei furniture maker stopped taking repair work in April for lack of finishing material, and the only kiln publishing a lead time quotes 2–3 months just to make the piece.
