A Takenaka fire-cladding certification just removed a floor-count ceiling on exposed-timber steel beams — but the certification number itself is still missing.

Takenaka's wood-clad fire-protection covering for steel beams, developed with two partners, secured a 3-hour-rated government fire-resistance certification. Under Japan's building code, buildings whose top 20-plus storeys require 3-hour resistance on primary structural members previously couldn't use wood-clad steel beams rated that high — capping exposed-timber construction below roughly 15 storeys from the top of a tower. This removes that ceiling, and a 2-hour-rated predecessor from 2023 is already built.

Worth treating carefully: the manufacturer's own page is bot-blocked, no certification number has actually turned up on any page that could be read, and an unverifiable "Japan's first" claim was downgraded rather than published as confirmed.

A Kyoto dealer-auction house's full commission structure is now on the record — both sides of the transaction.

Shibunkaku's own terms pages spell out exactly what buying and consigning through its quarterly sealed-bid sale costs: a tiered buyer's commission of 20% of hammer price up to ¥1M (blended 20%/15% above that), plus tax; and on the consignor's side a fee of 15% of hammer price, a fee of 3% of the minimum sale price if a lot goes unsold, storage insurance of 0.5% of the minimum sale price, and a catalogue placement fee of ¥6,000–¥50,000 by space — all plus tax.

Genuinely new detail on a sale already covered here for its sealed-bid mechanism — the fee schedule itself hadn't been published until now. A clean, dated procurement-margin comparator for anyone weighing this channel against a public hammer auction.

Yokohama's new OMO7 hotel occupies the old City Hall — and reconstructed a dead architect's missing typeface glyphs to caption the tile it preserved.

The hotel, open since April, sits inside the 1959 former Yokohama City Hall administrative building designed by Murano Tōgo, with Takenaka handling the renovation and Naruse Inokuma the interior design and supervision. Original glazed floor tile and a 1959 tile mural survive in situ in the ground-floor bakery, and a former council-chamber handrail was relocated and rebuilt into the atrium staircase rather than replicated.

One thing worth knowing before citing this project: an earlier pass had the wrong name for the typeface-reconstruction designer and wrongly called the preserved flooring wood parquet — it's tile, and the designer is Shiro Suzuki, both corrected from the primary sources this cycle.

Topcon renovated its 1935 concrete headquarters without ever stopping the factory running inside it.

The building — designed by the engineer known as "the concrete doctor" — reopened in February after a seismic retrofit and addition that also stripped away decades of added pipework and partition walls to restore the original open-plan volume. The whole renovation ran under a method the trade press names explicitly: kept-occupied construction, meaning manufacturing operated throughout with no shutdown.

Design supervision was by Kobori Tetsuo's studio. Worth filing as a named, documented precedent for renovating a live operating building with no closure window.

A retail brand, not an architecture studio, holds design-supervision credit across an entire Karuizawa complex and its embedded hotel.

Karuizawa T-SITE — six buildings, roughly 13,000㎡, 17 tenants, opened in March directly connected to the station — carries a single design-supervision credit for the whole complex: Conran Shop Japan. The same credit also covers the complex's 9-room boutique hotel, HACIENDA KARUIZAWA, embedded inside it.

One correction: the spa's "regional first" claim in an earlier pass overstated the source, which only claims a first store opening for that brand in the Kōshin'etsu region, not a first-ever open-air sauna. Worth noting as a precedent when a single design-supervision credit spans both a building's retail programme and a hotel inside it.

Last dossier

Japan's building code sets no statutory minimum illuminance for a hotel guestroom or a restaurant dining room — the only enforceable floor found anywhere is a business-licensing rule requiring more than 20 lux on the tabletop, and it only applies once a venue serves drinks past midnight. The Japanese lighting standard quietly deleted its old hospitality lighting tables in its 2024 revision; they now live in a different standard most spec sheets still cite by the withdrawn number.

Exit signage cannot be dimmed in any room serving alcohol, regardless of size — a real constraint late-night bars and lounges keep running into. Five named Japanese lighting-design practices publish their scope of work but not one publishes a fee, and the national design-fee standard doesn't mention lighting at all, so a client-appointed lighting designer's fee has no published benchmark anywhere. The one procured lighting-design price found in the entire search was a municipal nightscape-lighting budget, not a hospitality interior fee.
