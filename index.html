Japan's fire-retardant association just widened who can legally supply certified partition and acoustic panels — from 10 product categories to 14.

日本防炎協会 (JFRA) added four categories — disaster partitions, low partition panels, exhibition panels, and construction soundproofing panels — to the list a registered "simple-sewing operator" (a lighter registration tier than a full 防炎製品 manufacturer) may handle, effective June 1, 2026.

This is a standing rule, not a news event, so it doesn't stale: it's a real widening of the supplier pool for temporary partitions and acoustic treatments on hospitality and event-adjacent fit-outs, worth checking against any current or upcoming supplier shortlist.

Sendai is running a proposal for a mixed-use building that pays contractors to bring construction know-how into the design phase early — at a disclosed fee ceiling.

The ECI (Early Contractor Involvement) technical-cooperation commission for a complex near Kokusai Center Station carries a published 委託料上限額（事業費上限 税込）of ¥48,842,200円（消費税及び地方消費税を含む）, aimed at deepening design, rationalizing construction, improving quality, cutting cost, and shortening the build. Proposals are due November 13, contract expected January 2027.

A usable benchmark for proposing the same early-contractor-input structure on a complex hospitality or mixed-use project — an underused route with a real number attached.

A 56-metre timber-topped Ginza tower now outranks Tōji's pagoda — 380 years unbeaten — and the firm behind it held architecture and interior as one single commission.

On a site just 154.01㎡, 銀座髙木ビル SALON 91° rises to 1,341.83㎡ of floor area using conventional, non-certified timber construction, and the architect explicitly contrasted holding the whole scope — architecture and interior together — against the usual split into separate construction packages.

Two named finishes, BB Wood Japan's fire-treated cedar board and AICA's セラール decorative laminate, are both labelled 不燃 on the project page but carry no visible certification number — worth closing before either is specified on a real job. The height claim is independently corroborated by ArchDaily, not resting on one source alone.

A Nagano ski-rental shop simplified its roof because the local contractor said the original design couldn't be built in time — and finished every interior surface in unpainted particleboard to save cost.

The original L-shaped plan with a polyhedral roof was dropped after consultation with the local builder found it unbuildable within schedule; what replaced it is a form the area's precut supplier could build fast and that sheds snow well in a heavy-snowfall region.

Walls, ceiling and fixtures are finished in unfinished particleboard, laid running-bond, specifically to skip a paint step and hold the budget down — a real precedent for schedule-driven value engineering. The one open question is certification: nothing on the source page states a 不燃/準不燃 rating for this particleboard as a commercial wall/ceiling finish, so it can't yet be specified as fire-safe as-is.

Japan's own design-industry body says a bundled design-build contract still has to itemize design and supervision fees separately — it can't just say "included."

四会連合協定's published Q&A answers directly whether a bundled design-build contract can simply record design/supervision fees as "included in the construction contract": no — 設計施工一括であっても、設計業務と工事監理業務の具体的な報酬金額をそれぞれ明記する必要があります.

A citable, named-body basis for pushing back whenever a client or contractor tries folding those fees into one opaque construction number.

Last dossier

A backfill dossier on who actually performs 工事監理 on municipal building work found that the national dataset everyone had missed answers it directly: both routes — letting supervision to the designer, and doing it in-house — run inside most ordering bodies at once, selected per project, with third-party supervision the rare exception. No 中核市-level city was found publishing its own costing manual; the published tier stops at the designated-city level, which is itself the finding. The one hard number — the retained-work share a client keeps — is printed outright at 0.09, down from 0.12 and before that a range of 10.3–14.6%, but it applies to new construction only; renovation supervision has no published formula at all. The strongest line for a client room: the ministry's own guidance says that where the spec doesn't clearly state what's excluded, that retained share can't be set at all.
