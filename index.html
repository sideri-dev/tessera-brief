The Japanese Red Cross just opened a museum interior commission with a hard eligibility gate and price scored inside the proposal, not against a ceiling.

The Red Cross's own bid notice (dated 2026-08-31) opens a two-stage プロポーザル for roughly 440㎡ of interior and exhibition fit-out inside its Shiba-Daimon HQ, timed to the organization's 150th anniversary next year. To bid you need ten continuous years running a registered 一級建築士事務所, plus prime-contractor design-and-supervision experience on a built or in-construction 400㎡-plus museum or exhibition space completed since April 2016.

Scope is explicitly severed — interior and exhibition only, building and MEP procured separately — and there's no disclosed price ceiling: your quoted price is submitted as part of the proposal and scored alongside design quality at an October 16 hearing. That shape — hard track-record gate, scope carve-out, price-inside-proposal — is worth mapping even for practices not bidding this one.

A new law makes 手形払い illegal for design subcontracting and turns cost-escalation renegotiation into a legal entitlement.

取適法 (中小受託取引適正化法), in force since January 1 and confirmed against the regulator's own 324-page Q&A guidance, explicitly covers 情報成果物作成委託 — including "各種デザイン" — whenever the commissioning party clears a capital-or-headcount threshold (over ¥50 million capital, or over 100 employees where the capital test doesn't apply) against a smaller counterparty.

Article 5 bans payment by promissory note (手形払い) and anything not convertible to cash within 60 days, and creates a duty to renegotiate price "regardless of the size of the increase or decrease" when costs move. Confirmed across three independent outlets plus the regulator's own text — this changes what a larger client can lawfully do subcontracting design work downstream, and gives a smaller practice a statutory hook rather than a negotiating ask when costs move mid-contract.

A 1972 mountain hotel just proved how long unrenovated hospitality fabric can actually last before it forces a closure, not a refresh.

Hotel Tateyama, at Murodo station, 2,450m elevation, ended overnight lodging on August 31 after 54 years with no substantial interior renewal — original chandeliers and opening-era furnishings reportedly still in place on the last day. Architect was 村田政真.

Hoshino Resort bought the property in 2024 and, per both sources checked, has explicitly not committed to a renovate-versus-rebuild plan; the restaurant and shop continue while former operator 立山黒部貫光 redirects resources toward transport and F&B/retail. A rare, named durability window for hospitality interior fabric under real alpine conditions, and a live example of an acquiring operator declining to commit — useful precedent either way in a client conversation about how long a spec has to last.

A cleanly disclosed, benchmarkable fractional-hospitality pricing structure, and a heritage-adjacent coastal renovation with a named design brief worth holding as a reference point.

The 不燃認定 dossier finds the fork for natural materials written into the statute itself: 法2条9号 splits into a free, listed 告示 route versus a paid 大臣認定 route (¥520,000–¥1,620,000 per application), and that fork has moved twice recently — 壁土 (mud wall) crossed to the free side in 2022 for an explicitly hospitality-driven reason (converting an earthen-wall building into a shop or hotel), while a kitchen exemption route closed in 2020 by writing hotel, ryokan and restaurant kitchens out of it by name.

Fee mechanics reconcile to a cheapest complete new certification of ¥539,500, or ¥929,500 with the required gas-toxicity test — a flat add-on that penalizes exactly the unstandardized natural materials (earth, straw, untreated timber) most likely to vary between specimens. A 2011 audit found nine of ten sampled non-combustible-wood certifications sold against out-of-spec product across 344 buildings, and all nine remain live and unrevoked today.
