A new MLIT notification lets small hospitality builds use 3D-printed-formwork concrete under ordinary building-permit review, no bespoke ministerial certification required.

A new ministry notification generalizes a route this page first reported in August, sourced then only to a construction-industry blog — today's finding replaces that with the primary government document itself, seal and signature intact, and every governing threshold: single storey, footprint capped at 200㎡, ceiling height to 3.5m, a minimum concrete strength, and specific minimums for wall thickness, floor slab and wall-beam depth.

That envelope is the whole finding — inside it, a kiosk, pavilion or small outbuilding can be specified in printed-formwork concrete on a standard building permit; outside it, the older bespoke-certification route still applies. Worth carrying into any small-footprint hospitality outbuilding brief where the technique is on the table.

Japan's subcontractor-fairness law now has a named clause for the exact pitch structure design studios run — soliciting several concepts and buying only the winning one's IP.

The Fair Trade Commission's own published guidance spells out "unjustified low-pricing" as a distinct violation from the payment-timing rules already covered here, and its worked example — a client asking for multiple design proposals, picking one, and taking IP transfer on that basis — describes a structure many studios treat as normal unpaid pitch practice.

The statute gives a designer a named basis to push back on a price set unilaterally, before a fee is agreed, rather than negotiating from nothing.

A city museum design competition put a hard ¥62,546,000 tax-inclusive ceiling on the whole proposal — a usable comparator for scaling a private cultural-facility fee.

A Japanese city's own procurement notice states the figure as an all-in proposal ceiling — a single cap, not a scored price range — and three firms competed for it. It's older than the usual recency window, but earns its place as a dated public-sector budget comparator rather than a claim about current pricing.

Exactly the kind of number worth having on hand when scoping a private museum or cultural-space fee proposal against a public benchmark.

Imported structural glulam shipments fell again in the first half of 2026 — the second straight half-year decline, and volumes stay below 300,000㎥ for a second year running.

A Japanese timber trade paper's own customs-derived figures put H1 2026 imports at roughly 247,000㎥, down 4.1% year-on-year — a different metric from the monthly import series already tracked here, confirming the same direction: tightening supply.

Anything specifying imported structural glulam on a live project should treat lead time as a live risk, not a formality.

A Karuizawa wellness property's hinoki-based scent turns out to be commissioned from a named, independently-verifiable scent-design practitioner — a real vendor, not just atmosphere copy.

The original claim that the scent runs throughout the whole facility doesn't hold up against the source text, which describes a calming hinoki-based scent in specific zones, with the sauna instead using ambient forest air.

What does hold up: the practitioner behind it, credited as a leading figure in aroma-design and independently profiled by two unrelated outlets — a genuine, checkable name to call for a scent-design commission on a hospitality project, even if the "whole facility" framing was oversold.
