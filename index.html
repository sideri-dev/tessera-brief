Today

Japan's Supreme Court just handed down its first clear test for when a mass-produced design object can hold copyright — and denied one to a famous children's chair.

The ruling (2026-04-24) replaces the old "independent aesthetic appreciation" test: a mass-produced object's form is copyrightable only where it reads as creative expression separate from its function. Applied to the TRIPP TRAPP chair, of which 14 million units have sold worldwide, the Court found form and function inseparable and refused copyright — confirmed on two independent legal commentaries, both citing the court's own website.

For commissioned furniture and object forms, design registration is now the intellectual-property route to prioritise over copyright.

A live fee-calculation tool is quietly running the numbers on Japan's defunct architect-fee standard, with no warning that it's dead.

bldg-calc.com runs a working calculator for Japan's superseded 2019 architect-fee standard beside the current one, with nothing on the page saying the old standard lost legal force in January 2024. The page's own publish date is March 2024 — two months after that supersession — so it launched already wrong and was never corrected.

Check any fee figure sourced from the old standard against this exact trap before it enters a negotiation.

A 1964 city hall by Sakakura Junzo has reopened as a 19-room hotel and Japan's first "stay-in" public library — with the original architect's furniture drawings reissued by their original maker.

Hakusen, in Iga, opened this April: Tendo Mokko remakes furniture from Sakakura's own drawings, and board-formed concrete sits beside preserved original formwork, ash wood and plaster. Ceramics are by Taro Tsubota, with a mosaic mural by Masaomi Yasunaga. Attached is Iga's central library, confirmed open — roughly 3,600 square metres and 230,000 volumes, marketed as Japan's first stay-in public library.

A clean, reusable route for a mid-century concrete heritage brief: reissue through the original maker plus the original architect's own drawings, and leave the formwork visible.

A fresh auction result gives a real, dated spread between what a Nakashima chair set and a Kuramata chest actually clear right now.

At Phillips' Design Online sale (concluded 2026-09-10), a Nakashima "Grass-Seated" chair set, estimated $10,000–15,000, sold for $23,220 — 155% of the high estimate. A Kuramata "Dinah" chest, estimated $4,000–6,000, sold for just $4,128. Both sold-for figures are hammer plus premium, the house's own convention.

A current, correctly-typed acquisition-cost benchmark — and proof that different makers' estimates don't move together.

There's now a named, stock-held domestic alternative to imported sauna wood — from the only Japanese maker licensed by Finland's own technical research centre.

Koshii Lumber is confirmed as the sole Japanese company holding a VTT (Finland) licence and Thermowood Association membership for thermally-modified timber. Its SAUMO hinoki carries stated span limits (300mm at 15mm thickness, 350mm at 18mm), uses no chemicals, and stays in stock rather than made to order. A dated installation in July 2026 confirms it's live in the market now.

No fire-certification status is stated anywhere on the maker's page — a real specification option, but an open certification question before it goes into a fire-rated room.

Last dossier

No private hotel operator's interior brief is public; every document read for this dossier is a public-sector analogue, and that gap is itself the finding. The two full accommodation briefs read fix programme, key count, area and cost cap but leave the interior to one adjectival sentence, filing furniture and fittings as a purchasing line with no designer named — while a comparable public-servant-housing brief spells out actual fire-finish requirements, so the gap is about who carries the risk, not sector maturity.

Scoring weights design as the largest evaluation block, yet room count outweighs the only interior-quality line four to one, and a published Q&A exchange lets the interior get decided later, at working-design stage, by change order rather than in the brief itself. This also corrects an earlier reading here: interior scope isn't automatically "additional work" under Japan's fee standard — extra fee only arises from an explicit special client request. What's fixed before a designer is ever engaged is nearly everything with a number attached, while a furniture-and-fittings budget as a share of revenue, an official per-key construction cost, or any real case of interior design itself being the commissioned service, could not be found anywhere in public record.

This week

The fee table is being withdrawn from above while museum-scale commissions get priced from below by a disclosed ceiling with zero price weight. The ministry's own guideline now recommends abolishing or simplifying the fee-calculation table it published, naming each firm's own pre-declared rate rule as the intended replacement — while two live public museum commissions this week priced exactly the scope that table never covered, publishing a tax-inclusive ceiling with price scored at zero. The corrected fee-stacking figure to quote going forward is a 19.7% increase, not the 7.6% figure that had circulated.

Certification verification has moved down a level, from "is it fire-rated" to "which register row, which route." A manufacturer published a wrong certification number for its own product on its own website; a separate finding showed the certification-symbol family has six variants rather than three, one of which reads as fire-safe but is unspecifiable on an interior wall; and where no certificate exists at all, the substitute is a named statutory route that needs written confirmation early, before a wall build-up is fixed.

Every craft and furniture supply channel priced this week publishes no public terms, and the only new floor under any of it is a labour-subcontracting statute now catching every named contract-furniture house. In force since January, it requires design and intellectual-property scope to be priced separately from concept work, or the buyer risks a statutory "buying too cheap" violation — a rule cutting both ways on a design practice's own paperwork, both what it pays makers and what it charges clients for concepts.
