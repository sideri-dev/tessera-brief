Japan's lighting-design society has given its top award to a renovation method that measures old light instead of redesigning it.

When the Ebara Hatakeyama Museum reopened in October 2024 after a renovation and new annex (2,935.49 square metres total), the design team measured the building's pre-renovation illumination and colour temperature, then simulated those readings back into the new lighting plan rather than starting from a fresh fixture layout. The new wing's tile is slip-cast ceramic fired by reduction; the main hall keeps its original black-plaster finish.

A genuinely reusable method for any heritage or renovation brief where the goal is matching an existing atmosphere rather than upgrading it — confirmed independently by both the awarding body and the design-team firm.

Domestic Japanese structural lumber just hit its highest price since the "wood shock," and the cause is regional, not national.

An industry association's own September report names heavy rain in Tochigi prefecture as having damaged forest roads and disrupted log transport, pushing sugi pillar material to its highest price since the post-2021 wood shock. In neighbouring Gunma, mills are sitting on full log inventory and deliberately restraining collection to avoid insect-damaged stock, with rising fuel costs delaying kiln-drying on top.

If you're specifying domestic softwood structure right now, budget for a live, regionally-driven price spike rather than a national trend.

Japan's last maker of the paper that holds gold leaf together has a six-month backlog and no one to hand the workshop to.

A 220-year-old hand-papermaking workshop in Tsuyama, Okayama, is reported as the sole remaining source for the interleaving paper gold-leaf production depends on; sales sit at roughly a third of their level 30 years ago. This is a single-point-of-failure fact for the entire domestic gilding supply chain.

Anything specifying gold-leaf finishes should treat the six-month wait as a real lead-time constraint to plan around now, not a curiosity to note later.

One Tokyo architecture firm has published its actual hotel-and-museum fee table, tier by tier.

A named Tokyo firm's five-tier fee schedule puts hotels, museums, clinics and multi-family housing in the same class, with published bands running from 6.0–8.6% of construction cost at the ¥30,000,000-and-below tier down to 3.9–5.7% at the ¥2,000,000,000-and-below tier — consumption tax excluded, building-confirmation fees included, development-permit fees billed separately.

A named, dated, firm-published percentage table scoped to exactly hospitality and cultural work, usable as a direct comparator in a fee conversation rather than a generic industry rumour.

A Tokyo urushi maker is now selling a full-height lacquer door as a stock product, price included.

Launched August 2026, the door is hand-finished in over 15 coats of genuine urushi lacquer using a traditional wood-grain technique, with an option for Morioka cast-ironware hardware, starting at ¥2,210,000 (tax excluded) per unit in sizes from 590–1,100mm wide by 2,000–3,000mm tall.

Genuinely orderable — a real spec option for a hospitality entrance or feature door — but the release states no fire, F☆☆☆☆, or building-code certification, so that gap needs closing before it can go anywhere code applies to the door assembly.

Last dossier

The corpus's deepest look yet at vintage Japanese design furniture finds the market is far less liquid than it looks: the only standing Japanese auction for this material deletes every catalogue page once a sale closes, so a public Japanese clearing price for any of four major mid-century designers exists for about two weeks and then vanishes. Abroad, seven auction venues publish four genuinely different numbers all called a "result" — hammer alone, hammer plus a fixed multiplier, hammer plus a stated premium, hammer plus fees and taxes — so nothing compares house to house without first converting to a common basis.

Only one of the four designers' work is genuinely liquid with a real price history; two others have appeared in a Western sale a combined total of three times, so for them a current list price exists but no public clearing price does. Every maker's current production undercuts the vintage dealer ask, so the only honest reasons to buy vintage rather than new are a discontinued material or a genuine one-off. No piece in the whole study carries a flame-resistance certification — and none is legally required to, since neither the fire-safety nor building-code lists reach seating.
