Christie's own sale page for the 181-print Medaugh collection (New York, September 15, 2026) names the top results: Buyo Kanazawa hassho yakei (Eight night views of Kanazawa) realised $345,600 against a $40,000–60,000 estimate (8.6x low), Kameido ume yashiki realised $358,400 against $60,000–100,000 (6.0x low), and Awa, Naruto no fuha realised $281,600 against $30,000–40,000 (9.4x low) — all "price realised," Christie's own term for hammer-plus-premium.

A primary-verified benchmark for how hard top-tier ukiyo-e clears against estimate right now, usable for a wall-art acquisition budget on a hospitality guestroom or lobby program.

A former art museum at the foot of Yamadera (山寺, Yamagata) reopens October 1, 2026 as a biometric-driven light and scent installation — still unbuilt as of this page.

"照応｜SHO-O," produced by T&S Corporation's THINK AND SENSE and operated by 名湯一門高見屋, with 両足院副住職 (vice head priest) 伊藤東凌 as concept advisor, uses visitors' smartwatch vitals to drive real-time light and video across six rooms. The main hall runs 3.4m high by 13m wide, and one room pairs bespoke seasonal (春夏秋冬) fragrances with photographic panels. Reservation-only, 15 timed sessions a day, ¥2,000 general admission.

A scoped precedent: biometric light plus bespoke scent commissioned as its own line item inside an adaptive-reuse cultural building, not folded into general finishes.

Andersson Bell's first Japan flagship keeps its 1993 boardmarked concrete fully exposed rather than re-cladding it.

I IN (Yohei Terui, Hiromu Yuyama) fitted out the Korean brand's 309m², three-storey Tokyo Aoyama store inside an existing 1993 building, opened August 1, 2026. Boardmarked concrete stays raw throughout; contrast comes from custom furniture and steel pipes that read as piercing through the existing structure, a glass floor, and a red-to-yellow colour progression rising through the floors.

A clean adaptive-reuse retail precedent: keep a raw 1990s shell as the material statement, and do all the design work in fabricated insertions.

Japan's plywood market is showing early softening — shipments down, inventory up — and Yokohama has started restricting imports on capacity grounds.

JAWIC's (Japan Plywood Inspection Corporation) own September 2026 industry report shows domestic plywood production flat month-on-month (100%) while shipments fell to 87.8% of the prior month and inventory rose to 111.8% — makers are holding stock they aren't moving as fast. New this cycle: imported-plywood arrivals reached 19.4万㎥ (194,000m³) in July, and rising inventory at major Kanto ports has pushed Yokohama specifically into import intake restrictions and port-acceptance limits from capacity overload.

Rising stock against soft shipments hints at near-term price easing even as list prices hold; the Yokohama constraint is a fresh, dated lead-time risk specific to imported plywood.

Last dossier

Both Southeast Asian tax treaties are dead weight on a royalty: the Japan–Thailand cap (15%) and the Japan–Singapore cap (10%) each exactly match that treaty's own domestic royalty rate, so the only real lever is whether a design fee is contracted as a service or a licence. Thailand is harsher on every other axis too — a shorter permanent-establishment threshold and an exemption that fails once the payer is a Thai client rather than a Japan resident.

Licensing bites before tax does: Thai interior work on public buildings of 500m² or more requires Thai domicile and a work permit with no ASEAN route in, while Singapore leaves interior design itself unregulated but voids an unauthorised supplier's fee entirely once a submitted plan is involved.

This week

A certificate's conditions, not its existence, are the real spec. Two dossiers this week found a "fully certified" fire cladding actually rates the beam only, not the column, and a metal siding marketed as spec-ready is held by a subsidiary, valid on one face, with no true fire-rated certificate — closing it out of real fire-zone hotel builds. Check who holds a number and which face it covers before the brand's own page.

The fee standard doesn't price interior or art-direction work — three mechanisms create that line anyway: a qualifier written into the brief, a separately contracted appointment, and a priced IP clause.

Adaptive reuse is this week's dominant deal shape, now with named methods: a light-reconciliation technique for renovating old lighting, a reissue route through the original maker plus the original architect's drawings, and the corpus's first published building-conversion fee ratio.
