Today

A Neyagawa team-housing complex rebuilt its community hall with bamboo louvers residents are meant to replace themselves.

Students at Osaka Electro-Communication University, working with Osaka's housing-supply corporation and Neyagawa City under an MLIT demonstration programme, renovated the 香里三井団地 community hall and let residents vote on the final design from student-drawn options. The deck's bamboo louvers were previously used as a prop at local festivals and are built to be swapped out by residents themselves roughly every ten years — the material choice is explicitly about the maintenance ritual, not performance. It's thin as findings go — no floor area, no budget, no named designer beyond "students" — but it's a real, dated precedent for a community-buy-in maintenance strategy on a renovation, single-sourced to one press release.

Last dossier

The dossier traces a 道の駅 (roadside station) rebuild's construction-budget chain in Fukuyama from the city's original ¥14.9億 basic plan through the winning proposal's ¥21.6億 figure toward the 2027 tender — and finds the city's own procurement notice was deleted from its site rather than moved, leaving only a freedom-of-information request as a route to confirm who actually set that number. The winning proposal document itself hides an earlier, different cost breakdown beneath its surface figures, with every line below the top number having moved between drafts except the ¥21.6億 itself — circumstantial evidence the figure is city-issued, not yet provable. Construction supervision for the project hasn't been let and structurally can't be until FY2027: a sweep of 641 bid results since FY2022 found only one architectural supervision contract on record anywhere in the city, a ¥799,000 job from 2017, so any forecast fee for that stage would be guesswork against the published standard. The eventual construction bid will clear in a different percentage band than the design fee did — the city's own building-works tenders cluster at 91.5–92.3% of the ceiling with five-plus bidders, a mechanical output of its minimum-price formula, not a market signal. Two small corrections land along the way: the access road is prefecture-managed, not national, and this city's tender notices print their ceiling price on the face of the document rather than withholding it.

This week

The percentage-of-construction-cost fee claim died three times on primary-source review.

Japan's 2024 fee standard never actually prices design work as a percentage of construction cost; a municipal "roughly 4%" design-plus-supervision ratio in Fukuyama turned out to be circular math, already four weeks stale against the real signed contract by the time it was reported; and a detailed sheet-count study recovered the real governing unit as a yen-per-A1-drawing-sheet rate, all-in. The practice move: never argue fee as a percentage of cost in a client room — argue building type times hours, or sheet count, with the rate's edition and tax basis both named.

Craft capacity keeps getting counted, and the digital fallback keeps failing to show up.

Toyama's wood-lathe tradition is down to nine working craftspeople, nearly all over 70, and the CNC alternative that might relieve pressure on hand craft generally turned out this week to be unproven on strength, unavailable at temple-grade scale, and unpriced anywhere public. Hand-cut specification now has to name a workshop and its calendar, not just a material; a contractor's "we'll machine it and save" claim carries the burden of proof on strength, supply and price before it's taken at face value.

Four unrelated certification mechanisms converged on the same failure this week: the paperwork that's supposed to govern a material in use often isn't inspected, isn't published, or doesn't exist as a route at all.

Spray-foam fire-stop compliance has no inspection checkpoint anywhere in the system; a traditional timber joint has no certification route by law, full stop; and a wood-exterior fire certificate is technically live but functionally uncallable by anyone outside the reviewing body. The fix is the same every time — the compliance route has to be written into the designer's own drawings and acceptance clauses, because nowhere else in the system reliably holds it.
