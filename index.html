A small-island hotel in Kagawa was designed, by brief, to convert into staff housing — and the architect reversed the usual courtyard logic to make it feel like island life, not a resort.

Tsubame Architects built Hotel Shu as four two-storey timber buildings around a shared courtyard for an agritourism operator on Toshima Island (population ~750, over half elderly), who asked for something closer to an island house than a commercial hotel. The architect's own framing: the garden reads more public than the alley — "a reversal of public and private" — the opposite of how a resort courtyard usually works.

Named material spec, transferable: cedar siding, 大栄窯業 roof tile, black-and-white 漆喰 plaster, LIXIL aluminum sash, exposed structural plywood ceilings, マーモリウム linoleum — though the plywood and cedar flooring carry no stated 不燃/準不燃 fire rating, so neither is specifiable as fire-safe yet.

Design-firm bankruptcies in Japan just hit a 37-year high — and the failures cluster hardest in interior and furniture design specifically.

Tokyo Shoko Research counted 41 design-industry bankruptcies in the April–September 2026 fiscal half, up 86.3% year-on-year and the first time the figure has topped 40 since 1989; 39 of 41 had under five employees. Of the seven largest by debt, five mainly did housing-interior or furniture design — a sharper counterparty-risk signal than "small design firms generally."

Last dossier

A backfill dossier re-checked Japan's rules on traditional organic roof coverings (thatch, cedar shingle, cypress bark) inside fire-prevention zones and found the premise dissolves: a roof-covering-only re-roof generally isn't even a "major repair" under the relevant ministry notice, so the existing-building exemption that would otherwise be lost almost never triggers. Across every fire-spread safety certificate ever issued, exactly one organic roof covering has been certified — a cedar-shingle-over-plywood system — and zero for thatch, cypress bark or cedar bark. The two real precedents that keep a thatched or bark roof legal in a zoned area don't comply with the rule; they relax or exempt it, and both pay for that with water — a monitor cannon or river-fed pumps plus two firefighting reservoirs.

This week

A city's published "ceiling" turned out to be the wrong figure, by 13%. Shiga's museum extension carried a widely-cited ¥724M ceiling that a dossier traced to a budget-authorization line, not the fee; the real design-fee ceiling is ¥631M — the fourth such mislabelling this corpus has caught. Check a ceiling against the tender document itself, never a press release.

Hospitality's biggest paying work right now is conversion, and the fee standard has no row for it. Three built precedents landed in one week (a hostel rebuilt suite-by-suite, a former prison turned 48-suite hotel under a justice-ministry PFI, a heritage hotel's preserved-vs-new room typology), while regulatory research confirmed renovation design has no published fee formula and that touching a roof alone can forfeit a fire-safety exemption. Price renovation on hours, not the percentage table.

A "traditional" craft designation says nothing about its supply chain. Shiga designated a basket-weaving craft with two makers and zero backup workshops the same week Kyoto designated two crafts carrying 127 and 44 working makers. Same designation, opposite capacity — pull the maker count from the record before specifying.
